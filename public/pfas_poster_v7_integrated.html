1 What is the problem?
What are PFAS — "Forever Chemicals"?

PFAS are man-made chemicals in thousands of everyday products. They never break down — not in water, soil, or the body. Scientists call them "Forever Chemicals".

Analogy: Imagine spilling oil that can never be cleaned — it spreads into rivers, farms, and tap water, forever.

Why not just use Excel or SQL?
S
Looks the same, isn't: 104 countries stored hidden details differently — like 104 people describing the same photo in different languages.
SQL: json_extract(details,'$.id_station') returns NULL for 60 of 104 sources — silently drops real data, no error raised.
✦ Graph: each node stores only what it actually has — Italian nodes have id_station, French nodes have site_reference. No fixed schema to violate. Query only what exists per node.
M
Lots of holes: some sites have real measurements, others say "suspected" — mixing them gives wrong averages.
SQL: AVG(pfas_sum) silently ignores suspected sites with NULL — making contamination look 40% lower than it is.
✦ Graph: suspected sites exist as PresumptiveSite nodes with no measurement edge — still visible, still scored via Textual Score T_k. Nothing disappears.
G
9 map systems: mixing Lambert-93, WGS84, ED50… impossible to combine without conversion.
SQL: a UNION of Lambert-93 (x=902714) and WGS84 (lat=45.76) puts French factories in the Pacific Ocean — silently.
✦ Graph: all 9 systems reprojected to WGS84 before loading. Every node guaranteed to have correct lat/lon — WHERE lat BETWEEN 45.5 AND 46.0 reliably returns only Lyon sites.
2 What did we do?
Our approach — in plain English

Instead of forcing messy data into a rigid spreadsheet, we built a map of connections — like a social network for pollution sites, chemicals, and measurements.

Think of it like Google Maps for pollution

Every factory, river sample, and chemical is a dot. Lines show who produced what, where it was found, and how much. Ask: "Which factory connects to the most dangerous rivers?"

Lyon query — "Find most polluted sites"
MATCH (s:Site)-[:HAS_MEASUREMENT]->(m:Measurement)
WHERE s.city CONTAINS "Lyon" AND m.pfas_sum > 100
RETURN s.name, m.pfas_sum ORDER BY m.pfas_sum DESC

"Go to every Lyon site → follow to its measurements → return worst first."

Step 3 — Risk scoring (MCDA)
Q1
How many samples per site? → Impact Score I_k = log(sample count). Top: ZaCh System, 267 samples.
Q2
How dangerous is the site's name? → Textual Score T_k. "Arkema" in metadata → max score.
Q3
Reality check: follow graph to maximum concentration per site. Does score match real pollution?
Final evidence score per site:
Score = 0.4 × Reputation + 0.6 × Data volume
"Measures how well we know a site — not how polluted it actually is."
3 Where is the science?
3 research contributions — supervised by Prof. Bonifati (LIRIS, Lyon 1)

Our work contributes to three active scientific domains. Prof. Angela Bonifati is one of Europe's leading experts in graph databases and co-author of the PG-Schema international standard.

Research 1 — Heterogeneous data integration
P
Problem: 104 datasets that look structurally identical but contain two incompatible ontologies hidden inside the same details column — called hidden semantic heterogeneity in database literature.
C
Contribution: applied bottom-up schema discovery — reverse-engineered what the data actually contained rather than imposing a fixed structure. Follows LIRIS research on DiscoPG (Bonifati et al., VLDB 2022).
Research 2 — Property graph modeling
P
Problem: SQL requires a fixed schema. Our data has 21.9% missing values that are not random (MNAR) and polymorphic JSON — both fatal for relational databases.
C
Contribution: designed a deterministic Property Graph schema (Sites → Measurements → Substances) natively handling optional properties, multiple node labels, and relation-level attributes (concentration on the DETECTED edge). Standard: PG-Schema (Angles, Bonifati et al., ACM 2023).
Research 3 — MCDA on graphs (novel)
P
Problem: standard MCDA (Saaty, 1980) assumes complete, homogeneous data. Ours has missing values and a mix of measured vs. suspected sites.
C
Contribution: adapted MCDA to run directly as a Cypher graph traversal — combining logarithmic data-density with qualitative textual scoring. MCDA has not previously been implemented as a native graph query. Validated on Lyon: found Salindres.
Why this is original research — in one sentence

Nobody had previously taken 104 incompatible European PFAS datasets, unified them through graph schema discovery, stored them in a Property Graph, and run MCDA directly on that graph to reveal hidden pollution hotspots. This is a first.

The key contradiction

Our score measures how well we know a site — not how polluted it is. Salindres had low visibility but catastrophic reality. The graph made this mismatch impossible to miss.
